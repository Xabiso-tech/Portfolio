Studio note

01 — Curiosity

It started with making marks

Before any of this was software, it was paper. Graphite and charcoal, mostly, in a house in Khayelitsha where the light was never quite right and you learned to work with what you had. I drew because I wanted to, and I kept going because drawing is honest about how long things take.

What I did not expect was how much structure it would teach me. A face is not a face, it is a set of planes and edges that happen to line up. A shadow is not one colour, it is a gradient you have to earn stroke by stroke. When something looks wrong you cannot argue with it, you just erase and go again. That instinct turned out to be the single most useful thing I brought into programming.

02 — Learning

Then the work got structured

Drawing is self-directed, which means it is also easy to avoid. I needed something with deadlines and other people holding me to them, so I went into structured coursework: HTML and CSS first, then JavaScript, then the parts I had no idea existed, like SQL, PHP, Vue and Node.

Most of it was unglamorous. Conditionals, loops, functions, arrays, the DOM, error handling, template literals, scope. I did them badly, then redid them. My GitHub is mostly small exercises, and I am not going to pretend otherwise. But you cannot hold up a framework you have not built something small with, and the small things are the ones I remember.

03 — Experimentation

Projects that taught me the rules by breaking them

The Workshop is where the real learning sat. An image gallery that taught me grid and lazy loading. A product store that taught me why consistent spacing matters more than any colour choice. A booking form with a validation bug I did not find until months later.

Each one looked finished right up until it did not, and each failure was specific and fixable. That is the thing nobody tells you about learning to build: it is mostly diagnosing, not inventing. You get very good at reading an error message and forming a theory about what it means, then being wrong, then forming a better one.

04 — The part that changed direction

Then I had to make the front end talk to a real database

This is where the portfolio stopped being a front-end exercise. On a four-person HR system project I took the employee and performance review modules to the back end: Express routes, controllers, models, MySQL tables, and a JWT sign-in flow, so the pages I had built on the front end were reading live records instead of a JSON file someone had typed in by hand.

The second team project, WeConnect, tested that on a much bigger scale. It is a B2B marketplace where small businesses order stock from wholesalers, and my part was delivery: the orders, payments and GPS tracking back end, and the pages that drew it on a map. Six hundred commits by four people, and a database behind a managed host that had to be reached over an encrypted connection. Reading somebody else's work is a skill of its own, and it is one I only learned by being buried in it.

It was a different kind of problem. A bad layout is visible immediately, so you fix it in a minute. A broken query is invisible until the page is empty for a reason you cannot see. Learning to think about what the data is doing, not just what the screen is doing, is the shift I am most grateful for.

I would still call myself a developer in training. But I know which side of the stack I am drawn to, and I know I would rather build the part nobody sees if it is the part that actually holds.

05 — Purpose

Why it matters outside the screen

I am not building this for a portfolio. I am in church every Sunday and I take that seriously, because what you believe about people shows up in how you treat them. Chess teaches me the same lesson from the other side: you lose gracefully, you do not fold.

In my community, gender-based violence and femicide are not statistics. They are people. I am involved in awareness work because the argument is simple: this is preventable, and prevention is somebody showing up.

The part I care about most is talking to younger boys. Not lecturing them, just being honest about treating women well, about safety, about what a healthy attitude toward someone actually looks like. A lot of that is just repetition, which is another thing art taught me before code did.

Building software is how I contribute to that. Systems that make it easier to do the right thing, or to notice when something is wrong, are worth more to me than anything that is merely impressive.

The shape of it

The journey so far

Curiosity, on paper

Years of graphite and charcoal, learning structure, proportion and how to correct a line.

Structured learning

HTML, CSS, JavaScript, then the languages I did not know I needed: SQL, PHP, Vue, Node.

Small projects

Gallery, store, booking form. Each one broken in a way that taught me something specific.

Back-end work

Employee and performance review modules in Express, with routes, controllers and models separated.

Databases

MySQL schemas, parameterised queries and delivery data behind a managed host.

Full-stack

Connecting Vue pages to the WeConnect API and drawing courier locations on a map.

Purpose

Building useful systems, continuing to learn, and showing up for my community.

Next

What I am building toward

A developer who can hold a whole system in their head, not just a screen. I want to be the person who can take a vague idea, work out what the data needs to look like, and then build the front end and the back end that agree with each other.

I am not there yet, and I would rather say so plainly than perform something I have not learned. What I can promise is that I keep showing up, I keep correcting, and I would rather build one thing properly than five things halfway.
